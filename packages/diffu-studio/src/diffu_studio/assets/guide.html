diffu · how a diffusion model writes by hand

From noise to handwriting — the map and the machine

Part I builds one picture — a map of image-space — and places everything on it: where real images live, where random points are, what training fills in, how sampling finds a new point, and where text and style act. Part I — the idea

One map, everything on it

1the goal · what the machine must do

Turn (text, style) into a handwriting image

Handwriting recognition (HTR) models learn from labeled pairs — an image of real handwriting next to its transcribed text. For historical Swedish archives, labeled pairs are scarce: huge collections are digitized, far less of it transcribed. diffu exists to manufacture more of them — given any text and a sample of someone's hand, generate a new image of that exact text, in that hand, real enough to train on. Two of the harder obstacles on the way, previewed now and solved later: text can be any character — rare marks, old spellings — that a model may barely have seen (§10 fixes this); and text has no fixed length, so the model has to know how much of a canvas to actually use (§12 fixes this).

So the output must be (1) legible and (2) controllable — exactly the text you asked for, in a hand you chose. Hold that contract; every design choice later exists to satisfy it.

The contract. Two inputs, one image out. Part I explains the middle box as geometry; Part II opens it as hardware.
before we startWhat's a "latent space," and why not just use pixels? An image is a grid of numbers. A latent is a much smaller grid that still captures the picture's essence — made by a VAE, whose full machinery (and exactly why the compression is worth it) is §9. diffu never actually touches raw pixels: every "image," every map, every point drawn in Part I below is really a latent. We'll keep saying "image" because it reads easier — carry one substitution with you from here on: image → latent, until §9 makes it official.
The same word, actually shrunk — not just described. Left: "Göta" as this page always draws it. Right: the identical picture, averaged down 8× per side (diffu's real VAE ratio, §9) — the same blob of ink, in a fraction of the cells. Below: a VAE doesn't just shrink — it re-describes each of those small cells with 16 numbers instead of 1 (3 shown, tinted, illustrative); more numbers per cell, far fewer cells. Why bother: the ~24-times-repeated attention (§6) has to look at every cell, every single time — shrink the grid and every one of those 24 passes gets cheaper, which is the entire efficiency argument; exact counts, the frozen/fine-tuned split, and the legibility gate that guards this compression are §9.
2from step 1 · the space every image lives in

Every image is a point — here is the space, and where the random points are

Two classic pictures, and this whole project stands on both. First: plot a huge pile of MNIST digits as points — one per image — and project down to 2D. Images of the same digit cluster together: ten separate islands. Handwriting does the same — real "Göteborg" images cluster into their own island, distinct from "Lund"'s. Here's the part that matters: an island is almost impossibly small next to the whole space. A real image has thousands of independent directions; a valid island only uses a handful of them (slant, weight, spacing…). Nudge a point in almost any of the thousands of other directions and you don't get a slightly different word — you get static. Nearly this entire space, everywhere you're not standing on an island, is just noise.

Second classic picture: diffusion is a real physical word — and now it's the same process shown twice, not two different ideas side by side. Right: drop ink into still water and it doesn't stay a drop — it diffuses, spreading outward until the glass is a faint, uniform, structureless tint. Left: the identical thing, happening directly on the map — pick any island (they're all the same story; click one to make it the example) and watch its point spread outward (blue) until it's swallowed by the surrounding noise, indistinguishable from everywhere else, then find its way back. Drag the shared slider to drive both at once, or let them play. That's the forward process (§5): a real image (an island) diffusing into noise. The entire method is one question: can you run that film backwards? Learning to walk back from the noise onto the island, step by step, is training (§5) and sampling (§6) — not an analogy for them — and it's exactly where the name "diffusion model" comes from.

Both pictures are exactly true, just too big to draw — a handwriting line has ~10,000 pixels, so ~10,000 axes, shaped by thousands of rules, not one. Here are the same two claims, shrunk down until they're small enough to check with your own eyes:

step 2a · an image IS a point — check it with two pixels

Why start with two pixels? Because the whole page rests on one claim: an image and a point are the same thing written two ways. An image is a list of darkness numbers, one per pixel; a list of numbers is a position. With two pixels the position lives on an ordinary 2-D plot — a world small enough to check the claim by hand. (Your real line-images are lists of ~10,000 numbers → positions in a 10,000-axis plot. Nothing changes but the axis count.) Drag the dot, and read the same two numbers on both sides:

First, just the identity. The dot's coordinates and the two darkness values are the same pair of numbers — move either, both move. That is the entire content of "an image is a point in a space". Then press "add a rule". Real handwriting obeys thousands of rules (strokes connect, letters have shapes, the baseline runs straight); two pixels can't carry those, so we use the simplest possible stand-in — "a valid image is a fade: pixel 2 = 65% of pixel 1" — just to watch what any rule does to the space: the valid images collapse onto a thin line. Off the line = rule broken; the arrow back is the fix, one nudge per pixel, its coordinates printed on the right.
your question, answered"Why two pixels?" Because a claim should be checkable before it's scaled. Two pixels ⇒ two numbers ⇒ a fully drawable plot where you verify with your own eyes that image and point are one object, that a rule makes the valid set thin, and that the fix-arrow's coordinates are the per-pixel nudges. Ten thousand pixels is the identical statement with more axes — verified small, trusted big.
your question, answered"What even IS the map-view spline?" Exactly this plot, scaled past drawability. A handwriting line has ~10,000 pixels ⇒ ~10,000 axes instead of 2. And instead of one fade-rule it obeys thousands (strokes must connect, letters must have shapes, the baseline must run straight…) ⇒ the valid images form a thin curved surface instead of a thin straight line. We can't draw 10,000 axes, so from here on every map shows a 2-D shadow of this plot — same dot-is-the-image, same thin valid set, same off-means-corrupted, same arrow. Positions and distances keep their meaning; the axes don't.

step 2c · who knows what, when — dots, then arrows, never the band
all that exists: the training images
Who actually has the band? Nobody — ever. Step through the stages. Before training, the only thing in existence is a finite pile of real images — the dots. No band, no arrows. (If the band were known, no training would be needed: you'd just project noise onto it and be done. The whole method exists because it isn't.) After training, what exists is the dots plus a compass: a function that at any point says which way realness lies — the arrows, and nothing more. The band is never computed or stored by anything. It's drawn in these figures as the implied target — the crest where all arrows converge and shrink to zero; in the hills view below, the arrows are literally that ridge's "uphill".
your question, answered"Is the whole spline known in the beginning? And how do we get new points from it?" It is never known — not at the start, not at the end, not by us, not by the model. At the start there are only the dots (the training images). After training there are only the dots plus the arrows. So new points cannot be picked from the band — there is nothing to pick from — they are found by arrival: drop a random point anywhere (trivially easy: random numbers), ride the arrows until they shrink to nothing, and where you stop is, by construction of what the arrows learned, a point whose image looks real — including the infinitely many spots between the dots that were never on any list. The arrows exist between the dots because training pushed every dot off in thousands of random directions and amounts, carpeting the whole neighborhood, and the network is one smooth function that fills the gaps. The new image is discovered when you land, not selected from a catalog — the band is the destination set, revealed only on arrival.

Two roads from A to B — it plays by itself; grab t to drive. Road 1, the straight line: mix the pixels, (1−t)·A + t·B. Watch the top image: it's a ghost for the whole middle of the trip — never once a word. Road 2, along the band: turn the slant knob from A's value to B's. The bottom image is a real word at every single t. Same start, same end — but only the bent road stays inside "real handwriting" the whole way. A road of valid images between two valid images exists; it just isn't straight. That is the entire reason the band is drawn curved.
your question, answered"Why are the bands curvy 'splines'?" Two data facts, not decoration. Thin: a written word has only a few knobs — slant, weight, spacing, letter shapes — not 10,000 independent pixel-knobs; few knobs ⇒ a thin surface, and sliding along it turns a knob (in these maps: slant). Curved: the straight road between two real images is a ghost road (watch t above); a road of valid images exists, but it bends — and a set whose straight chords fall outside it is, by definition, curved. The specific S-wiggle is schematic — the true surface lives in ~10,000 dimensions and can't be drawn — and it's the same curve in every map on this page on purpose, so you're always looking at the same space. What's faithful is exactly three things: thin, curved, continuous. (That real images concentrate on such a thin surface is the manifold hypothesis — an empirical regularity with very strong support for natural images, not a theorem.)
step 2e · the hills are the same object, tilted
tilt noise level t side view — the probability hills
Here are your hills — and the dynamic you were expecting. Height = how probable a real image is at that spot: one long ridge along the band (tilt to 0° = the flat map used everywhere on this page); beads = the training images. Now drag the noise slider: adding noise doesn't move anything — it melts the landscape. At t≈1 the hills are spread completely flat (pure noise: every point about equally likely); as t falls they re-sharpen onto the band, and "closer ⇒ more probable" becomes literal — probability concentrates onto the ridge and pulls the walk home. This melting family of landscapes IS diffusion: the arrows of §4–5 are the uphill of the current t's landscape — which is exactly why the model takes t as an input (§10 — the modulation door). (§8 reshapes this ridge per writer; nothing ever moves sideways. The ridge is drawn with width — the ideal density is razor-thin; widening is exactly what the noise slider does.)
your question, answered"Where are the 3-D probability hills — and why a thin curve instead of a round cluster?" Same object, two cameras: the hills are the side view of the probability, the band is its top-down footprint (tilt to 0° above). And the footprint is thin because a round blob would claim that every direction away from a valid word stays valid — but only a handful of knob-directions do (slant, weight, spacing…); the other ~9,990 pixel-directions instantly make it a non-word — §3's noise slider is one of them. Few valid directions ⇒ no volume ⇒ a thin ridge, not a ball. (The round blobs you may know from t-SNE/UMAP plots are squashed 2-D shadows of exactly such thin surfaces.)
3from step 2 · defining "off the manifold", visibly

On vs off the manifold — distance = corruption

One slider drives both views: it pushes the image off the band and shows what that does to the pixels. This is the literal definition of "off the manifold": not looking real — and how far off = how corrupted.

image view · the same point, as pixels
map view · the same point, in the space
push it off → ON the manifold — looks real
ON = looks like real handwriting. A little off → NEAR (grainy, readable). Further → OFF (breaking up). Far → DEEP, then pure noise. The dashed line on the map is the distance; the image shows what that distance costs. Adding noise = walking away from the band; removing it = walking home.
The band is continuous — remember this for section 6. Slant, weight, spacing vary smoothly, so between any two real images on the band there are more valid images. The band isn't a set of dots; it's a surface you can land anywhere on.

5from step 4 · how the model LEARNS the arrow

Training — one skill: predict the corruption we added

This is the forward process — adding noise, on purpose, so its exact amount is known. We never teach it to draw. We corrupt real data by a known amount ("real data" here is already the VAE's compressed latent, §9 — never raw pixels) and ask it to name the corruption. Because we added the noise, the true arrow is known — so every guess can be graded and the model corrected. That grading is the whole objective: loss = ‖ model(noisy, t) − the true corruption ‖²

one training event · image view
the same event · map view — blue = true arrow (known) · orange = model's guess
One event, two views, in lockstep. A real image (a dot ON the band) is pushed off by noise we made — so the true arrow back is known before the model says anything. The model guesses the arrow — badly at first, watch the angle — gets graded against the truth, and nudged. Every graded guess teaches the arrow at one more spot, and crucially the model generalizes: the arrows fill in between the practiced spots too, until the whole field points home. That field is the trained model.
your question, answered"Where is 'the model learns to predict/remove the noise'?" Right here, and it's the only thing learned: input = (noisy image, noise level t) → output = the corruption in it (the arrow — diffu writes it as the velocity, §11a). Nothing else. Training starts from real data every time and never produces an image — its product is the arrow-field on the right.
Audit note — the exact bookkeeping. "The corruption" can be written three equivalent ways: as the noise ε, the clean image x₀, or the velocity v = ε − x₀. Given the noisy image x_t = (1−t)·x₀ + t·ε and t, each determines the others (x₀ = x_t − t·v, ε = x_t + (1−t)·v) — one skill, three notations. diffu's notation is the velocity (§11a); this page says "noise/corruption" where it reads easiest. Also, for full honesty: §7–8 add two more inputs to this same function — the text and the style — nothing else about training changes.
6from step 5 · how we USE the arrows to create

Sampling — how a NEW image is found

This is the reverse process — removing noise, one small step at a time, ~24 times in a row. Flip it. Start from pure noise — a random point in the space (already VAE-latent space, §9), one nobody ever used — and follow the learned arrows: predict, take a small step, repeat ~24×. The walk must end somewhere on the band. Which spot? Wherever that particular start flows to.

your question, answered"How are new points found?" Three ingredients, all already built: (1) the start is random — a point never used before; (2) the arrows are defined everywhere (section 5), not just at training dots, so any start can be walked home; (3) the band is a continuous surface (section 3), so "home" is generically a spot between training examples. Random start + arrows everywhere + continuous surface ⇒ a new valid image, every seed. The model learned the rules of handwriting, not a phrasebook of examples.
Keep these separate. Different inputs, different outputs, different jobs — training learns the arrow-field once; sampling walks it every time you generate.

7from step 6 · steering WHAT it says

Text conditioning — the text picks WHICH band the arrows aim at

As the faint neighbours in §2 promised: there isn't one band. Every possible text has its own thin band — a "Göta" band, a "Lund" band, … Unconditioned arrows just point to the nearest band: you get some word. Conditioning on a text = telling every arrow which band counts.

step 7a · what "conditioning" is — same point, different arrow
no extra input → the arrow aims at the nearest real-anything
Conditioning = handing the arrow-maker one extra input. Everyday version: standing on the same street corner, "where's the nearest restaurant?" and "where's the nearest Italian restaurant?" get different directions — same point, different question, different arrow. Step through: unconditioned (question blank) → nearest real-anything, the wrong word. Conditioned on "Göta" → the same model re-aims. The difference of the two arrows = the pull caused only by your text, with everything generic cancelled out. CFG = follow that pull ~5× harder (5 measured best on diffu's sweep; 3 was worse, 7 over-saturates). For the blank-question arrow to be trained, conditioning must sometimes be blanked during training. diffu's inference blanks the text only, style kept; the live 128-px run trains exactly that (--cond-dropout 0.1 --text-dropout 0.1 — sometimes everything blanked, sometimes only the text). Honest footnote: the released model trained only the everything-blanked null while inference asks the text-blanked one — a null mismatch the repo calls the CFG-null gap — and cfg 5 still measured best. The value: unconditioned you get a word; conditioned, your word; with CFG, legibly — and pushing k too far flies past the band, which is why ~5 is the sweet spot, not "more is better".

Style — the fingerprint reshapes WHERE on the band you can land (the hills)

Style enters as a fingerprint: a compressed summary of a writer's sample — slant, weight, rhythm — deliberately not their letter shapes (Part II §10 shows why). Think of "hills" as where landings are likely — literally the ridge height in §2's tiltable landscape.

Cycle the writer. No fingerprint: hills everywhere — any hand can come out. Writer A's fingerprint: the hills collapse onto A's stretch of the band (upright, even). Writer B: they move to B's stretch (slanted, heavier). A new fingerprint → the hills move somewhere new → a new hand, same text.

No fingerprint → bumps wherever training data sits. your question, answered"Are writer A and B spread over the whole band?" No — the whole band is every slant/weight of the word; a writer is a stretch of it. And keep the two directions apart, because they do different jobs: ⊥ distance to the band = how corrupted — walking closer only removes noise; ∥ position along the band = WHICH image — the style. New samples and new styles are new ∥ landing spots: text picks the band (§7), the fingerprint picks the tall stretch, the seed picks the exact spot.
Part II — the machine

The real diffu: what the boxes actually are

9one correction to part I · the space isn't pixels

The VAE — the space you've been looking at is a latent space

§1 already previewed why: the arrow-predictor runs ~24 times, so compressing first (8× fewer rows, 8× fewer columns → up to 256 positions instead of ~16,000) makes every one of those passes cheaper — worth a second look back if that's fuzzy. Here's the precise shape and the machinery behind it: the encoder turns the pixels into a small latent — 16 channels × 8 rows × (width/8) columns. All of Part I happened on latent points: the map, the band, the arrows, the walk. Only at the very end does the decoder turn the landed latent back into pixels.

diffu's VAE choices, and why. A 16-channel VAE (Qwen-Image) — high-capacity enough that thin strokes and diacritics survive compression. The encoder is frozen, so the latent space (the map!) never shifts under the model's feet. The decoder is fine-tuned on historical handwriting, guarded by the recon-CER gate: encode→decode held-out real Swedish, read it back with the HTR judge — decoded CER must stay within +5 percentage points of the raw-image CER (measured: +0.8 pp — pass). If å, ä, ö can't round-trip through the VAE, no diffusion model on top could ever write them — so that's verified before any training compute is spent. Value: the latent has 8× fewer rows and 8× fewer columns than the pixels — 64× fewer positions for attention to pay for, with 16 channels per position carrying the detail — the frozen encoder keeps the whole map from shifting mid-training, and the gate guarantees the alphabet survives compression before any training compute is spent.
So is the VAE itself a model — and where exactly does it run? Yes: a second, separate trained neural network (Qwen-Image's), doing a completely different job from the arrow-predictor. Two callbacks, now that you've seen it: in §5, "corrupt real data" meant VAE.encode(real image) → clean latent x₀, then corrupt that — never raw pixels. In §6, "pure noise" was already a random point in this same latent grid. The encoder runs once, only during training, to make x₀ in the first place; the decoder runs once, only at the very end of generation. Neither ever runs inside the ×24 loop — start to finish, that loop is 100% the other model, the MMDiT (§10's diagram below shows exactly where each one sits). Re-read Part I with one substitution: "image" → "latent" — the band, the arrow, the walk are all on the 16×8×(W/8) grid; only the VAE's decoder, right at the very end, turns the landed point into pixels.
10the box that outputs the arrow · two doors, one transformer

The architecture — glyph tokens, a DINOv3 fingerprint, and an MMDiT

The arrow-predictor is an MMDiT (the SD3-style diffusion transformer): 24 blocks, width 1024, 16 heads, ~0.9B parameters, trained from scratch. The design question is where each input enters — and the answer follows from what kind of information each one is. First, what a "door" even means:

your question, answered"Is the VAE split? Encoder after ResNet/DINO into MMDiT, decoder after MMDiT?" No — they never sit next to ResNet-18, DINOv3, or each other. They bookend two different moments. Below: the whole pipeline, drawn as those two moments, one on top of the other.
Two moments, one model pair, never adjacent. The VAE's encoder appears only in the top row (training, once per example); its decoder appears only in the bottom row (generation, once at the very end). The dashed vertical line is the point: it's the same trained MMDiT both times — a single forward pass during training, the identical weights run in a 24-step loop during generation. Neither VAE half ever sits beside ResNet-18 or DINOv3 — those process text and style, a separate stream that only meets the image inside MMDiT, never inside the VAE. The diagram below zooms into exactly what MMDiT reads on every one of those passes.
Two different 24s — don't merge them. "MMDiT × 24 blocks" (below) is the transformer's depth: 24 stacked layers inside one call. "Called ×24" above is how many times that entire stack gets invoked across the sampling walk (§6). They're unrelated hyperparameters that happen to share a number — diffu could run 40 sampling steps through a 24-block model, or 24 steps through a 40-block one, with no connection between the two. Concretely: generation runs the whole 24-block stack, start to finish, once per step — 24 steps × 24 blocks = 576 total block-passes, not "24 blocks split across 24 steps."
Two doors, not three inputs. Image and text both enter as tokens (Door 1) — literally concatenated into one sequence, so any position can attend to any other; that's what lets a patch look up which letter belongs at its own column. Style and the noise level enter as AdaLN modulation (Door 2) — one scale-and-shift, computed once, applied identically inside all 24 blocks; there's no position to point at. Swap them and it breaks: text as modulation could only whisper a vague "write something," with no way to say where each letter goes (why prompt-conditioned models misspell); style as tokens could let the model copy the reference's exact letter shapes instead of just its hand. 2 doors or 3 inputs? Both — count what you like: 3 kinds of thing enter (image, text, style+noise), through only 2 mechanisms — image and text happen to share the tokens door. Not assumed, measured: an early A/B pitted this token approach against a third option — channel-concatenating the glyph directly onto the latent instead — and tokens won clearly (gen-CER 0.73 vs 0.80, matched runs); at that checkpoint neither had fully learned to spell yet, which the released run's full training later fixed.
your question, answered"How do the VAE's 16 channels actually connect to MMDiT — is it cross-attention?" No — simpler, and it happens before any attention exists. Verified directly in diffusers' own code: the patch embedding is one nn.Conv2d(in_channels, 1024, kernel=patch, stride=patch) — a single learned matrix. A 2×2 patch × all 16 channels = 64 raw numbers goes in; one 1024-number token comes out. Every channel is folded in at this one step, exactly like a photo's 3 RGB channels would be — just 16 abstract "colors" per position instead of 3 literal ones. Only after this — once the latent is already a plain sequence of tokens with no visible "16" left anywhere — does joint attention start, and that's between these image tokens and the text tokens (Door 1, above), never between the channels themselves.
your question, answered"What's actually being trained here, what's frozen, and why?" Two different budgets, nothing blurred between them except one deliberate middle case:

Everything that has to learn something diffu-specific — reading a rendered glyph, picking a fingerprint out of raw features, and above all predicting the arrow itself — is trained from scratch, together, as one ~900M-parameter whole.

One part that looked needed and wasn't: an inference-time technique the repo calls "DOG guidance" shipped as the default for a while — until an honest sweep found its own error metric was silently broken, reporting ≈95% failure while masking that plain CFG (§11c) worked far better all along: the same model went from ≈51% error unguided to ≈14–16% with real CFG once it was actually tried. So why both Qwen-Image AND SD3.5 — isn't that two models? Two independent layers, sourced independently, for different reasons — not one model glued from two. The VAE contributes actual pretrained weights: Qwen-Image's real encoder/decoder, chosen because it's 16-channel (older 4-channel VAEs blur away marks smaller than 8px — å's ring, for instance), already tuned with text fidelity in mind, and permissively licensed with no access gate. SD3.5 contributes an architectural blueprint, not weights — diffu trains its own ~900M parameters from scratch inside that same transformer design (joint attention, qk-norm, the rotary position scheme) — the same design FLUX and Qwen's own diffusion models converged on too. Nothing from SD3.5's actual checkpoint loads into diffu. Swapping a VAE from one lab into a transformer designed by another is ordinary practice in this field: compression and generation are separable jobs, and here they're sourced separately on purpose.
What each box actually does — four mini-intuitions (illustrations). ① glyph → token: the Unifont bitmap of a character becomes a stroke-aware feature map (ResNet-18), then one 1024-number description of that shape, stamped with its column. ② resampler: DINOv3 hands back ~196 patch descriptions of the style crop; the Perceiver's 64 learned queries each ask a fixed question of them — variable-in, fixed-out. ③ attention-pool: one more learned query weighs those 64 and keeps the discriminative ones (slant, stroke width) instead of averaging — out comes the single fingerprint. ④ AdaLN: the fingerprint (+ t) becomes per-layer scale-and-shift pairs — a global tone knob: the same strokes, globally re-weighted, no positions touched.
Why draw the letters, instead of a normal embedding table? The usual text-model approach — one learned vector per character — only gets good at a character by seeing it often; rare marks (ſ, historical abbreviations, some å/ä/ö spellings) end up barely trained, and even common letters give the network just an abstract ID, not a shape — it has to reinvent "what does G look like" from data alone. The glyph door sidesteps both, which is why diffu can spell: the text is rendered with GNU Unifont (a bitmap exists for any glyph — å, ä, ö, ſ, historical letterforms, seen often or never), encoded by a small ResNet-18 — handing the network the shape directly, not an ID to decode. And — in the released model's glyph-line mode — it's laid out as one token per latent column, with shared-column 2D-RoPE: glyph token j carries the same column frequency as image patches in column j (Qwen-Image MSRoPE-style), so the image attends to the glyph at its own column and the text↔position alignment comes for free. The model never sees "the meaning" of the sentence — only which shape belongs where, which is exactly the job. (The repo's default for fresh runs is a simpler per-character token path; the release used the column-aligned line. Contrast with prompt embeddings in §12.)
Why DINOv3? Recognizing "whose hand" needs a good sense of how writing looks — slant, stroke weight, rhythm — without ever being told those words. DINOv3 already has that: trained on a vast pool of ordinary images with zero labels, purely by learning to describe crops of the same image consistently — a skill that transfers to handwriting it never once saw during its own training. Training an equivalent "style eye" from diffu's own archival data would need far more labeled style-similarity pairs than exist — so diffu borrows DINOv3's eyes wholesale, frozen, and trains only two small pieces on top to read them. And the style door is pooled on purpose — the repo has the receipts. The real pipeline: frozen DINOv3 → a small Perceiver resampler (64 latent tokens) → a learned attention-pool (one query cross-attends the tokens, so the pooled 1×1024 vector emphasises the discriminative cues — slant, stroke width — instead of averaging them away) → AdaLN. The repo also has a legacy mode that puts the 64 style tokens into joint attention — and documents exactly why the release doesn't: "the model copies the ref's glyphs and ignores the text — hold the style ref, swap the text → output unchanged." Per-position style = copyable "what"; the released model is pooled-only: "how", never "what". The cost is honesty in §12: styles come out somewhat generic.

11how it's trained well · three ingredients

The training recipe — rectified flow, REPA (with DINOv3), and CFG

Section 5 gave the objective. Three ingredients make it work fast and come out legible.

a · Rectified flow — straight paths, few steps

diffu predicts the velocity v = ε − x₀ of a straight noise→data path. Straight paths can be walked in ~24 big Euler steps instead of hundreds of small ones. This isn't the same fix as DDIM (2020) — DDIM speeds up sampling by reformulating how you walk the same curved path (skip steps, tolerate more error per step: ~1000 → ~20–50); rectified flow instead removes the curve itself, so a plain first-order Euler step needs less correcting to begin with. Different mechanism, and the straighter one is diffu's — inherited from adopting SD3's training recipe wholesale, not something diffu measured against DDIM directly (worth being precise about that; it's general lineage, not a diffu-specific ablation). The noise level t isn't drawn uniformly during training either: a logit-normal schedule practices hardest at middle noise — where the arrow is hardest and most informative (near t=0 it's trivial; near t=1 it's mostly guessing). Value: ~24 steps instead of ε-DDPM's 50–1000 — from ~2× up to ~40× fewer, depending on the baseline.
your question, answered"How does it 'know' which direction — it only ever sees a noisy point, never the real image that made it?" Two different things resolve this, and neither is magic.
First, why v = ε − x₀ at all. x_t = (1−t)·x₀ + t·ε is a straight line between two fixed points. Slide t from 0 to 1 and the direction you're moving never changes — it's the same everywhere on that one line, literally endpoint-minus-startpoint. That constant direction is ε − x₀. No mystery: it's just what "constant velocity on a straight path" means.
Second, and this is the real trick: the model was never shown ONE true direction for this point — it was shown MANY. A given noisy point could plausibly have come from several different real images (three shown here, faintly — each with its own true arrow, from its own training moment). The network doesn't pick one — trained on thousands of such overlapping examples under a squared-error loss, its provably best move is to output their weighted average, pulled hardest toward whichever real images sit closest. That's the solid arrow. Since real images — never noise — are what training ever shows it, that average, almost everywhere, points toward the nearest part of the band. That's "home" from §2 and §4 — arrived at by averaging, not by knowing.

b · REPA — borrowing DINOv3's eyes, early

REPA = REPresentation Alignment. During training only, layer-8's hidden state is pulled toward what a frozen DINOv3 sees in the clean target. The model gets mature "vision" almost for free, converges faster, and renders finer strokes; it's cut after ~40% of training (repa_stop_frac 0.4). At sampling time it costs nothing — the crutch is gone. Note the double duty: the same DINOv3 is also the style encoder (§10) — one foundation model, two jobs.
What REPA changes, concretely (illustration). Left: what a from-scratch model's middle layer "sees" early in training — mush, because it must invent stroke-vision and direction-home at the same time. With REPA, layer 8 is pulled to describe the image the way DINOv3 already does — stroke-aware from the start. Right: the payoff — a given quality arrives much earlier in training (representation-alignment papers report multi-× speedups; these curves are illustrative), and fine strokes (the dots and rings of å ä ö) survive. Then it's switched off at ~40% (repa_stop_frac 0.4): late training is about micro-detail, and forcing the match starts erasing exactly that. Value: compute saved, finer diacritics, lower CER.

c · CFG — the legibility lever at sampling time

guidance cfg = 4.0 · approaching the measured best (5)
Classifier-free guidance — §7a's two arrows, used together: at every step, predict the arrow twice — with and without the conditioning — and push along the difference: v = v_uncond + cfg·(v_cond − v_uncond). It exaggerates exactly what the text/style asked for. cfg ≈ 5 is diffu's single biggest legibility lever (measured sweep: gen-CER 0.25 at cfg 5 vs 0.33 at cfg 3; cfg 7 over-saturates). Inference blanks the text and keeps the style; the live run trains both nulls (--cond-dropout 0.1 --text-dropout 0.1) — the released model trained only the full null, and cfg 5 still won. (This preview is an illustration of the effect, not a live model.) Why a sweet spot: too low and the text's pull is a whisper (washy); too high and you overshoot the band on the far side (over-sharpened artifacts — push the slider past 5). Value: the single biggest legibility lever — the released model's ≈0–5% CER is measured at cfg 5.
12context · what diffu changes, and what it buys

diffu vs. standard Stable Diffusion

diffu adopts the modern SD3 recipe (MMDiT + rectified flow + 16-ch VAE) and then rebuilds every conditioning path for handwriting. The differences are the point:

What this buys

  • It can spell. Prompt-embedded SD famously garbles text; glyph tokens make the letters first-class citizens with positions — the difference between "about a word" and "this word, here".
  • Style is an independent knob — same text, any hand — instead of a vibe smuggled through a prompt.
  • Line-shaped generation: height fixed, width follows text — the shape HTR data actually has.
  • Validated by its consumer: the released 64-px model reads back at ≈0–5% character error rate at cfg 5 through Riksarkivet/trocr-base-handwritten-hist-swe-3 — an HTR model can literally read what it writes, which was the contract in §1.
  • Faster, finer training from REPA; ~24-step sampling from rectified flow.

What's honestly still hard

  • Length control is an auto-width formula (glyph natural width × 0.55 bias, rounded to 16, capped at 1024 px) plus a fill-ratio scalar folded into AdaLN and an ink-crop after generation — real signals, but no per-character length token, so extreme lengths can still pad or squeeze.
  • Pooled style is safe but generic: one 1×1024 vector can't clone a specific hand's quirks — that's the deliberate §10 trade-off.
  • Legibility leans on CFG: at low guidance the writing gets loose; cfg≈5 does real work — and the released model carries the CFG-null mismatch (trained full-blank, inference text-blank), which the in-flight 128-px run (exp_sd35_128, --cond-dropout 0.1 --text-dropout 0.1) closes.

Recap

Part I in one strip. A pile of real images → corrupt by known amounts and grade the guesses → the arrow becomes known everywhere (the compass) → from a random start, ride the arrows and land on a ✦ new valid image → text chooses which band counts, style which stretch of it. Everything below restates this in words.
  • 1 Goal: (text, style) → legible, controllable handwriting.
  • 2 Space: an image = a point; random points = static; real ones = a thin band (the manifold).
  • 3 Off the band = corrupted; distance = how corrupted; the band is continuous.
  • 4 The arrow: per-pixel correction pointing home — score = velocity = predicted noise.
  • 5 Training: corrupt real data by known amounts; learn to predict the corruption (diffu's notation: the velocity, §11a); the arrow becomes known everywhere. Creates nothing.
  • 6 Sampling: random start + arrows everywhere + continuous band ⇒ land between training examples — a new image per seed.
  • 7 Text: one band per text; the printed template, fed at every step, picks the band — that's the anchor.
  • 8 Style: the fingerprint moves the hills — which stretch of the band you land on; the seed picks the spot.
  • 9 VAE: all of it happens in a 16-ch latent space; frozen encoder, CER-gated fine-tuned decoder.
  • 10 Model: MMDiT with two doors (3 inputs, 2 mechanisms) — glyph + image tokens in attention; style + t via AdaLN; outputs the arrow.
  • 11 Recipe: rectified flow (straight, ~24 steps, logit-normal t) + REPA/DINOv3 early + CFG ~5 at sampling.
  • 12 vs SD: same modern chassis, rebuilt conditioning — that's why it spells, and why the style knob exists.

Words are really rendered and really corrupted; arrow-fields, walks, counters and the REPA/CFG previews illustrate the true high-dimensional dynamics rather than reproduce them.
