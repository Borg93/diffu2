How the pool pages are made, and the ones we are unsure of

Two archives of scans, 3.15 million in all, were read years ago by the old YOLO and TrOCR pipeline. We turn them into single pages a whole-page reader can learn from. First how that works, step by step, with one real scan followed through it. Then how far it has come in numbers, then real pages from each group we drop or doubt. Click any page to see it large.

How the curation works

1. Describe. Two image models (DINOv3 and TIPSv2) turn every scan into a list of numbers. Small classifiers, taught by your marks and by a vision-language model's answers on 100,000 scans, read those numbers and fill one table: what kind of scan, table or not, print or handwriting, which language.
2. Decide. We keep openings of handwritten running text. Tables go to their own pile for a table model later. Odd layouts, print and empty pages are left out.
3. Cut. The reader learns from single pages, so each opening is cut at its fold, found as the dark column between the two pages.
4. Read. Our own reader, Nemotron Parse fine-tuned on your ground truth, reads every page. That gives a second transcription next to the old label.
5. Check. Where the two transcriptions differ a lot, the old label is usually wrong. Pages where the reader loops, pages turned sideways and near-empty pages are taken aside.
6. Choose the label. The open question. Training on the old labels helped little (6.92 %). A blind review found our reader's text better, so the next test trains on the reader's own reading.
7. Train. Pretrain on the pool pages, fine-tune on the 49,491 hand-transcribed pages, score the same 200 test pages. Lower is better.

Two checks of the old labels, and what they actually catch

Both ask whether an old label fits its page, in different ways. The writing layer looks at the whole scan: a small model marks where there is writing (blue below), and we compare that with where the old pipeline drew its lines (red). It never reads the text. Jev works line by line: it gets one line's image and the label's text for it, draws the text, and judges whether the two match. It reads, but only one line at a time. Each sample has a number, so you can tell me which ones I judged wrongly.

Writing layer

My reading of the samples: where it finds writing with no label, it is right. The old pipeline skipped a whole page or column in all four. Where it finds a label with no writing, it is mostly wrong: the ink is faint or faded, the old lines are there, and the layer fails to see the pale writing. So only the first half is usable as a filter.

Jev

My reading of the samples: Jev is not reliable on these pages. Many lines it calls a mismatch (low score) look correctly transcribed, for example J1 and J6, while the true faults it finds are mostly fragments and struck-out text. Its training lines were cut by the ground truth's line outlines; here the old pipeline's rougher outlines cut them, which is a likely reason. On scans read by our reader it mostly repeats what the reader's disagreement already says. I would not use it as a filter.
